# Chapter One — The Other Phone

At half past eight on a wet Tuesday morning, M was explaining to a man in a tracksuit why a cracked screen was not evidence of a manufacturing defect.

The customer had placed the phone on the glass counter as though presenting evidence at a murder trial. The fracture ran from the top-right corner to the centre in a white spiderweb, the point of impact so obvious that M could have found it with his eyes closed.

“It was in my pocket,” the man said.

M turned the handset over once, carefully. “With what?”

“Nothing.”

“Keys?”

“No.”

“Coins?”

“No.”

“A set of house keys, maybe?”

“I just said no.”

M looked up.

He had learned very young that people often answered the question they wished they had been asked. The customer was in his forties, broad through the chest, rain still shining on the shoulders of his black jacket. He had come in ready for an argument. Some men carried anger the way other people carried umbrellas: not because it was raining, but because they expected weather everywhere they went.

M slid the phone back across the counter.

“There’s an impact mark here.”

The man leaned closer. “That was already there.”

“Before the screen broke?”

A pause.

Behind M, one of the younger staff looked down quickly to hide a smile.

The customer noticed.

“Something funny?”

“No,” M said.

He did not raise his voice. He almost never did. Volume was usually what people reached for when they had run out of better tools.

The shop was beginning to wake around them. Two fluorescent ceiling panels hummed faintly over the display wall. Handsets sat in acrylic cradles beneath printed cards promising minutes, data and upgrades. A woman in a nurse’s uniform waited by the accessories rack with a charger in her hand. Outside, the parade of shops along the Essex high street was still coming alive: shutters lifting, delivery vans blocking the service road, a bakery two doors down sending out the smell of warm bread that always made the shop seem colder than it was.

M wore a navy knitted polo, charcoal trousers and brown loafers polished enough to catch the light without advertising that anyone had polished them. The clothes were not expensive in the way his mother’s family would have recognised as expensive. They were simply right. Cut properly. Chosen carefully. Nothing screaming for approval.

The customer tapped the glass with one finger.

“So what are you saying?”

“I’m saying I’ll replace it at cost.”

“I’ve got warranty.”

“You do.”

“So replace it.”

“The warranty covers the phone failing. It doesn’t cover the pavement attacking it.”

The nurse laughed before she could stop herself.

This time even the customer almost smiled.

Almost.

“How much?”

M told him.

“That’s your cost?”

“Yes.”

“How do I know?”

“You don’t.”

The man stared at him.

M held the look without challenge.

After a moment, the customer exhaled through his nose, took out his wallet and said, “Do it.”

M handed the phone to Dev, who had been trying very hard to appear busy with a stock sheet.

“Forty minutes,” M said.

“An hour,” Dev replied.

“Forty.”

Dev looked at the queue forming near the door.

“Forty-five?”

M gave him a look.

“Forty,” Dev said.

The customer moved away.

M turned to the nurse.

“Charger?”

“Mine died.”

“They don’t die. People bend them until they give up.”

She smiled. “You always this cheerful in the morning?”

“Only with paying customers.”

He was reaching beneath the counter for the correct cable when the other phone vibrated in his trouser pocket.

Not the shop phone.

Not the number suppliers had. Not the number printed on invoices, loyalty cards or the window. Not even the number most of his friends used.

The other one.

He did not reach for it immediately.

That was another habit people mistook for calm. M had discovered that urgency was contagious. If you behaved as though something was urgent, everyone around you began treating it as important whether it was or not.

He sold the nurse the charger, gave a delivery driver directions to the back entrance, corrected a junior member of staff who had logged a trade-in under the wrong IMEI field, and only then stepped into the small office behind the stockroom.

The room had once been intended for two people. M had made it work for one by removing the second desk and replacing it with metal shelving. There was a kettle on a filing cabinet, three unopened supplier envelopes, a framed photograph of Kay holding Asha when she was a baby, and no certificates on the wall.

There were none to hang.

M had left school with nothing worth framing.

He took the phone from his pocket.

One message.

**TWO SIDES. ONE HOUR. THEY’RE BRINGING PEOPLE.**

No greeting. No name.

The sender did not need one.

M read it twice, not because the words were difficult but because the absence of words mattered too.

He checked the time.

8:43.

Then he opened a second message thread.

**Where?**

The reply came almost immediately.

**SOCIAL CLUB. EAST. 10.30. REECE SAYS THEY THINK HE TOOK IT. HE SAYS THEY DID.**

M leaned back in the chair.

There was a particular kind of stupidity that arrived when two groups of men had both decided they were victims. Facts became insulting. Questions sounded like accusations. Every concession became weakness. By the time weapons appeared, the original argument was usually the least important thing in the room.

His phone vibrated again.

**JAY KNOWS.**

That annoyed him.

Not because Jay knew. Jay almost always knew.

Because somebody else had decided Jay should know before M had decided there was anything worth knowing.

M called him.

Jay answered on the second ring.

“You awake?”

“It’s nearly nine.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

A pause. Then, “You’ve had the message.”

“Yes.”

“I was going to ring you.”

“No, you weren’t.”

“I was.”

“You were going to turn up here.”

Another pause.

M smiled despite himself.

Jay had been part of his life for so long that there were entire conversations they could have without speaking. They had grown up in Essex together, fought as boys, made money badly as teenagers, lost touch only in the technical sense when Jay joined the military and M stayed behind to build businesses no bank manager initially took seriously.

Years later, when Jay came back harder around the edges and quieter in the middle, very little between them had changed.

“You want coffee?” M asked.

“I want you not to go.”

“So coffee.”

“M.”

“I’ll see you in twenty minutes.”

He ended the call.

For several seconds he stayed in the chair.

On the shelf opposite him was a photograph of his mother standing outside the first shop on opening day. She was younger than he remembered her now, one hand raised to shield her eyes from the sun, the other holding a paper cup. The fascia above her still had the old logo, the one M had later changed because a customer told him it looked like a takeaway.

She had helped him with the deposit.

Not enough to make the business easy. Enough to make it possible.

His father had died so young that most of M’s memories of him arrived as still images rather than sequences: a quiet man tying a shoelace; laughing at something across a table; asleep on a sofa; a hand on M’s shoulder. His mother had filled the years after with movement. Work. Bills. Food appearing somehow. School meetings. Arguments. Pride disguised as criticism.

She had died only months before this morning, and M had discovered grief was less like a wound than a change in gravity. Everything still existed. It just weighed differently.

He turned the photograph face-down.

Not because he did not want to see her.

Because he needed to work.

When he stepped back onto the shop floor, Dev looked up.

“Everything all right?”

“Yes.”

It was a lie, but not a dishonest one.

Everything was often all right until someone made it otherwise.

M spent the next half-hour doing what the outside world believed he did.

He approved a refund.

He negotiated by phone with a wholesaler who had changed the agreed price of a shipment of handsets after the order had already been confirmed.

He showed a pensioner how to enlarge the text on her new phone.

He told one of the staff to stop putting premium cases beside budget handsets because aspiration did not work when the accessory cost half as much as the device.

He moved through the shop without hurrying.

People watched him.

They always had.

Not because he demanded attention. Quite the opposite. M had never understood men who entered rooms already performing themselves. Loud watches. Loud cars. Loud opinions. They spent so much energy announcing who they were that nobody had time to discover whether it was true.

M preferred discovery.

At nine-twenty the door opened and Jay walked in carrying two coffees.

He was English in the way M thought only English men could be: able to make a complaint sound like a weather report. Dark jacket, plain T-shirt, jeans, boots. Nothing military about him unless you knew what to look for.

Dev did.

Everyone eventually did.

Jay nodded at the staff and handed M a cup.

“No sugar.”

“You remembered.”

“I remember everything.”

“No. You remember useful things.”

Jay leaned against the counter.

“Same thing.”

For a moment neither spoke.

Then M said, “How bad?”

“Bad enough that I’m here.”

“That bad, then.”

Jay looked towards the office.

M shook his head.

“No need.”

“There is.”

“Then talk quietly.”

Jay studied him, then lowered his voice.

“Reece says forty grand is missing.”

“Forty?”

“Started at forty. Now apparently it’s sixty.”

“Money grows fast when angry men count it.”

“Other side says the package was light.”

“What package?”

“Doesn’t matter.”

“It matters if I’m expected to talk about it.”

“You’re not. You’re expected to stop them talking about it with guns.”

M took a sip of coffee.

It was good.

Jay always bought coffee from the same place because once he decided something worked he saw no reason to turn choice into a hobby.

“Who called you?”

“Reece.”

“Who called me?”

“Different number?”

M nodded.

That bothered both of them.

Jay's expression changed only slightly.

“Someone wants you there.”

“Obviously.”

“No. I mean wants *you* there.”

M looked through the window.

Across the street, rain had thinned into a fine grey mist. A bus pulled away from the stop and left an old man standing under the shelter reading a newspaper.

Normal world.

M had become increasingly interested in how little distance there was between it and everything people pretended did not exist.

A shop door.

A private phone.

A different table.

That was usually all.

“Vincent Kerr?” Jay asked.

M did not answer immediately.

Kerr's name had been around longer than M's in rooms like the one they were being invited to. He was not a gangster, which made him more useful to gangsters. He was the man people called when they needed a conversation to occur without admitting they needed it. Money. Debt. Introductions. Apologies. Agreements nobody intended to write down.

M had met him twice.

The first time Kerr had spoken to M as though explaining a menu to a child.

The second time he had been more polite.

M trusted the second conversation less.

“Maybe,” M said.

Jay watched him.

“That’s not a reason to go.”

“No.”

“Good.”

“It’s a reason to be careful.”

Jay closed his eyes briefly.

“M.”

“What?”

“You do this thing where you agree with me and then somehow arrive at the opposite decision.”

“I’m not going because of Kerr.”

“Why are you going?”

M looked towards the workbench where Dev was fitting the new screen for the man in the tracksuit.

“Because if two groups are ready to hurt each other over sixty thousand pounds, either somebody is lying or somebody is stupid.”

Jay waited.

“And?”

“And both are expensive.”

Jay gave him the look he had perfected over twenty years.

It said: you believe that sounded wiser than it did.

M ignored it.

At ten past ten, he put on a lightweight navy coat, checked the shop cameras from his phone and told Dev he would be out for an hour.

“Meeting?” Dev asked.

“Yes.”

“Business?”

M stopped at the door.

“All meetings are business.”

Outside, Jay's car was parked illegally.

M looked at the double yellow lines.

“You’re ex-special forces and this is what defeats you?”

“I’m here for ten minutes.”

“You said that last time.”

“I was here eleven.”

M got in.

Jay pulled into traffic.

For the first few minutes they said nothing.

Essex moved past the windows in damp fragments: terraces, roundabouts, builders’ merchants, coffee shops, new flats rising where old yards had been, men in vans arguing into hands-free kits.

M watched it all.

He liked Essex because it rarely pretended to be prettier than it was.

“You tell Kay?” Jay asked.

“I said I had a meeting.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“It’s what I answered.”

Jay glanced at him.

“You know she knows when you’re being evasive.”

“I’m not evasive.”

Jay laughed once.

It was the first genuinely funny thing either of them had said.

M's phone rang.

The other one.

Unknown number.

He answered.

A man's voice said, “You coming?”

“Yes.”

“Come alone.”

M looked at Jay.

Jay raised an eyebrow.

M said, “No.”

Silence.

Then: “That’s not how this works.”

M's tone did not change.

“It is today.”

He ended the call.

Jay drove another hundred yards before speaking.

“Good start.”

“He’ll call back.”

The phone rang again.

M let it ring.

Jay smiled.

“You enjoy this.”

“No.”

“You do.”

“I enjoy people discovering they have less control than they assumed.”

“That sounds healthier.”

M looked out of the window again.

Somewhere between the shop and the social club, the morning had changed shape.

It was still raining.

Customers would still be waiting.

A supplier would still be trying to increase his price.

Kay would probably message him about dinner.

Asha would come home from school carrying something she had made and expect him to understand immediately why it mattered.

All of that remained real.

So did the other thing.

The other phone.

The other rooms.

The other version of M that had begun, quietly, to become useful to men who had no shortage of dangerous friends and very few people they actually trusted.

M had not planned it.

That was the part nobody would believe later.

Legends were always rewritten backwards.

Once a man became powerful, people assumed he must have been building towards power from the beginning. They removed the accidents, the mistakes, the moments when he had simply said yes because saying no seemed cowardly or boring.

At thirty, M did not have a grand design.

He had three shops, a mortgage, a wife he loved, a daughter who believed he could fix anything, a dead mother he still expected to call, a friend beside him who had seen things M never asked him to describe, and a private phone that was ringing for the third time.

This time, M answered.

The voice was calmer.

“Bring Jay.”

M looked at his friend.

Jay nodded once.

M said, “We’re five minutes away.”

Then he ended the call and put the phone face-down on his knee.

Jay turned left towards the social club.

Neither of them knew yet that by lunchtime, one man would be lying, another would be bleeding, sixty thousand pounds would cease to be the important number, and Vincent Kerr would hear M's name spoken in a room where it had never belonged before.

M only knew one thing.

Somebody had gone to considerable trouble to make sure he attended.

That meant the meeting was not the problem.

He was.
